\repsection{Stations}{Readings at fixed stations}

\begin{frame}{Sampling the displacement at fixed stations}
\begin{columns}
\column{0.7\textwidth}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[rep, height=6.4cm, xmin=0, xmax=1, ymin=-1.5, ymax=1.6, xlabel=$x$, ylabel=$u(x)$]

\stepfrom{2}{\addplot[only marks, mark=triangle*, mark size=3pt, sblue] table[x=x, y expr=-1.42]{data/readings5.dat};}
\stepfrom{3}{\addplot[muted, dotted, line width=0.8pt, ycomb, mark=none] table[x=x, y=u]{data/readings5.dat};
          \addplot[only marks, mark=*, mark size=2.2pt, sred] table[x=x, y=u]{data/readings5.dat};}
\stepfrom{4}{\addplot[sblue, line width=1.8pt] table[x=x, y=v]{data/segments5.dat};}
\stepfrom{5}{\addplot[only marks, mark=none, error bars/.cd, y dir=both, y explicit, error mark=none, error bar style={syellow, line width=3pt, opacity=0.75}]
          table[x=x, y=v, y error plus expr={max(\thisrow{u}-\thisrow{v},0)}, y error minus expr={max(\thisrow{v}-\thisrow{u},0)}]{data/gaps5.dat};}
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\column{0.3\textwidth}
\stepfrom{3}{\chip{five readings}{$0$, $1.11$, $0.60$, $0.31$, $0$}\\[6pt]}
\stepfrom{5}{\chip{worst miss, five sensors}{$0.22$, or $20.1$ percent}}
\vfill
\live{readings-at-fixed-stations}
\end{columns}
\note{
Here is a taut string, pulled sideways.
The whole curve is its displacement, and we want to store it in a computer with a handful of numbers.
How?

The obvious move is to measure it.
Put five sensors along the string, at $x = 0$, $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac34$, $1$.

They give us five readings, $0$, $1.11$, $0.60$, $0.31$, $0$.
Everything between the sensors is unknown.

So we guess.
The simplest guess connects neighboring readings with straight lines, and we have a curve again.

Now look where the guess is wrong.
The lines miss the bends between sensors, and the worst miss is $0.22$, about a fifth of the largest displacement.
Would more sensors fix this?
}
\end{frame}

\chip{$m = 5$, $h = 1/4$}{20.1 percent}\\[4pt]

\note{
So we add sensors.
Before adding any, guess how fast the miss should fall.
Between two sensors a distance $h$ apart, the curve bends away from a straight line by about $u''h^2/8$ at the middle, so halving $h$ should cut the miss to a quarter.

Nine sensors give $6.3$ percent, a factor of $3.2$ and short of four.
At five sensors one segment spans more than a third of the shortest wavelength in the curve, so the bend inside it is far from a parabola and the estimate $u''h^2/8$ is too crude.
The estimate applies once $h$ is small against that wavelength.

Seventeen sensors give $1.6$ percent, a factor of $4.0$, and from here on every halving divides the miss by four, a slope of $-2.07$ on logarithmic axes.
We can draw these gaps only because we know the curve.
What else do the readings alone hide?
}
\end{frame}

\column{0.3\textwidth}
\stepfrom{2}{\chip{change in the seventeen readings}{$0$}\\[4pt]

\vfill

\note{
Suppose all we have is these seventeen readings.
Which curves could have produced them?

Take one.
Add the wave $0.3\sin(16\pi x)$ to the displacement.
At every station $16\pi x_i$ is a multiple of $\pi$, so the wave is zero there and not one reading changes, while the curve moves by $0.3$ at each crest, about a quarter of the largest displacement.

Two functions that agree at every station are aliases on that grid, and the readings cannot tell them apart.
Could a few numbers describe the whole curve at once, instead of one place each?
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Representing a function with coefficients on sine modes}
\begin{columns}

\begin{tikzpicture}

          \addlegendentry{sum}}
\stepfrom{5}{\addplot[only marks, mark=*, mark size=2.4pt, ink] coordinates {(0.5,0.8) (0.5,0) (0.5,-0.2) (0.5,0.6)};
          \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=west] at (axis cs:0.52,0.8) {$0.8$};
          \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=west] at (axis cs:0.52,-0.2) {$-0.2$};
          \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=west] at (axis cs:0.52,0.6) {$0.6$};}
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\column{0.3\textwidth}
\stepfrom{4}{\chip{the whole curve}{three numbers, $(0.8, 0.4, 0.2)$}\\[4pt]}
\stepfrom{5}{\chip{check at mid-span}{$u(\tfrac12) = 0.8 - 0.2 = 0.6$}}
\vfill
\live{coefficients-on-shapes}
\end{columns}
\note{
Here is a different idea.
Instead of asking where the string is, ask what it is made of.
The string vibrates in sine modes, and the plot shows the first one weighted by $0.8$.

Add the second mode, weighted by $0.4$.
Add the third, weighted by $0.2$.

Add the three together and the displacement of the string appears.
Three numbers, $(0.8, 0.4, 0.2)$, give the whole curve, with no grid anywhere.

Check it at mid-span.
The second mode vanishes there, the first and third read $1$ and $-1$, so $u(\tfrac12) = 0.8 - 0.2 = 0.6$.
And since every sine vanishes at both ends, every curve we can build this way is pinned.
}
\end{frame}

\begin{columns}

\begin{figure}

\note{
Look at what the three numbers are.
On the left, $(0.8, 0.4, 0.2)$ is an arrow with three components.
On the right, the same three numbers are the displacement.

Add two displacements and their coefficients add, scale one and its coefficients scale, so the family is closed, the three modes are a basis, and a displacement is a point in a three-dimensional space of functions.
}

\begin{columns}

\column{0.3\textwidth}

\note{
So far every curve was smooth, because every sine is smooth.
Now press on the string at one point.
The point load bends it into two straight segments with a corner at mid-span.

Before we can say which sum is closest, we have to say how we measure an error.
}

\repsection{Norms}{Norms and the meaning of error}

\begin{frame}{Measuring error in the sup, $L^2$, and $H^1$ norms}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.33\textwidth}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[rep, height=5cm, xmin=0, xmax=1, ymin=-0.3, ymax=0.3, xlabel=$x$, title={\scriptsize error $e = u - v$}]
\addplot[name path=E, ink, line width=1.2pt] table[x=x, y=e]{data/error5.dat};
\addplot[name path=Z, draw=none] {0};
\addplot[sgreen!45] fill between[of=E and Z, split, every segment no 1/.style={fill=sred!45}, every segment no 3/.style={fill=sred!45}, every segment no 5/.style={fill=sred!45}];

\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\chip{largest gap, sup norm}{$0.22$}
\column{0.33\textwidth}
\stepfrom{2}{\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[rep, height=5cm, xmin=0, xmax=1, ymin=0, ymax=0.06, xlabel=$x$, title={\scriptsize squared error $e^2$}]
\addplot[name path=E2, ink, line width=1.2pt] table[x=x, y=e2]{data/error5.dat};
\addplot[name path=Z2, draw=none] {0};
\addplot[syellow!70] fill between[of=E2 and Z2];
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\chip{root of the area, $L^2$ norm}{$0.093$}}
\column{0.33\textwidth}
\stepfrom{3}{\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[rep, height=5cm, xmin=0, xmax=1, ymin=-3.2, ymax=3.2, xlabel=$x$, title={\scriptsize slope error $e'$}]
\addplot[sorange, line width=1.2pt] table[x=x, y=de]{data/error5.dat};
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\chip{root-mean-square slope, $H^1$ seminorm}{$1.20$}}
\end{columns}
\vfill
\hfill\live{coefficients-on-shapes}
\note{
The error of the five-sensor guess is $e = u - v$.
How large is it?
That depends on what we care about.
If it is clearance under the string, only the worst gap matters, $0.22$, and that is the sup norm.

If it is the overall misfit, all the gaps count at once.
Their average fails, because positive and negative gaps cancel, so square first, integrate, and take the root.
That is the $L^2$ norm, $0.093$.

If it is the elastic energy, the values are the wrong thing to look at, since the energy is half the integral of the squared slope.
The root of the integrated squared slope error is the $H^1$ seminorm, $1.20$, and half its square is the energy of the error, $0.72$.
In relative terms the values are off by $14$ percent and the slopes by $42$.
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Disagreement between the sup, $L^2$, and $H^1$ norms}
\begin{columns}[T]

\stepfrom{3}{\begin{tikzpicture}

\column{0.4\textwidth}
\chip{spike, base $0.04$}{sup $1$, $L^2$ $0.115$, energy $50$}\\[4pt]
\stepfrom{2}{\chip{spike, base $0.01$}{sup $1$, $L^2$ $0.058$, energy $200$}\\[4pt]}
\stepfrom{3}{\chip{$L^2$ distance, spike to shifted spike}{$\sqrt2 \times 0.115 = 0.163$}\\[4pt]
\chip{wiggle $0.05\sin(40\pi x)$}{sup $0.05$, $L^2$ $0.035$, $H^1$ $4.44$}}
\vfill
\live{coefficients-on-shapes}
\end{columns}
\note{
Can the three disagree by a lot?
Take a spike of height one on a base of width $0.04$.
Its sup norm is one.
Its $L^2$ norm is $\sqrt{b/3} = 0.115$, and its slopes are $\pm 2/b$, so its elastic energy is $2/b = 50$.

Quarter the base.
The sup norm stays at one, the $L^2$ norm halves to $0.058$, and the energy climbs to $200$.
No multiple of the $L^2$ norm bounds the sup norm, and a displacement of finite energy cannot contain a spike of zero width.

Shift the spike by one and a half base widths, so it overlaps the original nowhere.
The $L^2$ distance between the two is $\sqrt2 \times 0.115 = 0.163$, because the $L^2$ distance compares the two point by point and they never overlap.
A small fast wiggle, $0.05\sin(40\pi x)$, is small in the sup and $L^2$ norms, $0.05$ and $0.035$, and its slope swings to $\pm 6.3$, so its $H^1$ seminorm is $4.44$.
}

\begin{frame}{Definition of a norm and a seminorm}
\begin{block}{Norm}
\[
\|u\| = 0 \text{ only for } u = 0, \qquad \|\alpha u\| = |\alpha|\,\|u\|, \qquad \|u + v\| \le \|u\| + \|v\| .
\]
\end{block}
\medskip
\begin{block}{Seminorm}
\[
|c|_{H^1} = \Bigl(\int_0^1 (c')^2\,dx\Bigr)^{1/2} = 0 \quad \text{for every constant } c, \qquad\text{and on the pinned string the only constant is } 0 .
\]
\end{block}
\note{
We have been calling three different rules a size.
What do they share?
Each gives the zero function size zero and nothing else size zero, each scales with the function, and each gives a sum at most the sum of the sizes.
A rule with these three properties is a norm.

The slope rule misses the first property, since a constant function has zero slope, so on its own it is a seminorm.
On the pinned string the only constant allowed is zero, and there the slope rule is a norm after all.

So the phrase closest member of a family means nothing until we name the norm.
Name $L^2$, and ask which sum of three sines is closest to the shape of the string under a uniform load, the parabola $x(1-x)$.
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The projection theorem}
\begin{block}{Projection theorem}
In a space with an inner product, a finite-dimensional family has one closest member to each target, and that member is the one whose error is orthogonal to the whole family.
\end{block}
\bigskip
\[
\langle u - v, \phi_j\rangle = 0 \quad \text{for every shape } \phi_j, \qquad c_k = \frac{\langle u, \phi_k\rangle}{\langle \phi_k, \phi_k\rangle} \text{ when the shapes are orthogonal.}
\]
\note{
Orthogonality gives one linear equation per shape, so the closest member is computed and never searched for, and when the shapes are orthogonal to one another each coefficient is a single division.
The Fourier coefficients of a function are these divisions, one per mode.

For a family with infinitely many shapes the theorem needs two more hypotheses, a complete space and a family that contains its own limits.
Is the inner product itself fixed, or a choice?
}
\end{frame}

Flip $n$ coins that land heads with probability $x$, and weight the reading $f(k/n)$ by the probability of exactly $k$ heads.
Each hill is that probability, peaked at $k/n$.

With eight coins the hills narrow and the sum closes in.
The gap at the corner is the average distance of the fraction of heads from one half, about $0.4/\sqrt{n}$, which predicts $0.141$ against the measured $0.137$.

With thirty-two the gap is $0.070$ against a prediction of $0.071$.
The hills narrow like $1/\sqrt{n}$ and so does the gap, and it always sits at the corner, where the function is least smooth.
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bernstein basis polynomials}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/mlp-bernstein-approximation}
\end{center}
\[
B_{k,n}(x) = \binom{n}{k} x^k (1-x)^{n-k}, \qquad B_n f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} f\!\left(\tfrac{k}{n}\right) B_{k,n}(x)
\]
\centering
\chip{largest gap, $n = 5, 10, 20, 40$}{$0.222$, $0.117$, $0.060$, $0.030$}\quad
\chip{the basis}{nonnegative, sums to one, peaked at $k/n$}
\note{
Give Bernstein's construction the smooth target $\sin(\pi x)$.
The basis shapes $B_{k,n}$ are nonnegative and add to one at every $x$, so $B_n f$ is a weighted average of the readings $f(k/n)$, and each shape peaks at its own reading's location $k/n$.

An average of readings of a concave function sits below it, so every $B_n f$ lies under the sine, and the gap at mid-span is largest.
The largest gap is $0.222$ at $n = 5$, $0.117$ at $n = 10$, $0.060$ at $n = 20$, and $0.030$ at $n = 40$, so each doubling of $n$ roughly halves it.
The convergence is uniform, which is the Weierstrass theorem proved by construction.
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Weierstrass approximation theorem and its limits}
\begin{block}{Weierstrass (1885)}
For every continuous $f$ on $[0,1]$ and every tolerance $\epsilon > 0$ there is a polynomial $p$ with $|f(x) - p(x)| < \epsilon$ at every point of the interval.
\end{block}
\medskip
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.5\textwidth}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[rep, height=4.4cm, xmin=0.35, xmax=0.65, ymin=0, ymax=0.2, xlabel=$x$, legend pos=north east, title={\scriptsize $B_{32} f$ beside the corner}]
\addplot[ink, dashed, line width=1.2pt] table[x=x, y=f]{data/bern32.dat};
\addlegendentry{$|x - \tfrac12|$}
\addplot[sblue, line width=1.6pt] table[x=x, y=B]{data/bern32.dat};
\addlegendentry{$B_{32} f$, slope $0$ at $\tfrac12$}
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\column{0.5\textwidth}
\chip{Bernstein's error}{falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$}\\[4pt]
\chip{best degree-$n$ polynomial}{falls like $1/n$}\\[4pt]
\chip{the theorem names}{no polynomial, no degree, no slope}
\end{columns}
\note{
So polynomials come as close as we like to every continuous function.
Notice what the theorem does not say.
It names neither the polynomial nor its degree, and it says nothing about slopes, since every $B_n f$ has slope zero at the corner where the slope of $f$ jumps from $-1$ to $1$.
Bernstein's error falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$, the best degree-$n$ polynomial does better, like $1/n$, and the theorem names neither.

Density is also different from having a closest member.
Polynomials of a fixed degree contain their closest point, and the polynomials of all degrees have no closest member for the corner, since the distance can be made smaller than any positive number and is never zero.

Universal approximation theorems for neural networks have the same form and the same silences.
}
\end{frame}

\draw[->, thick, muted] (s) -- (c) node[midway, left, font=\scriptsize] {sigmoid units\ };
\draw[->, thick, muted] (c) -- (h) node[midway, below, font=\scriptsize] {any bounded nonconstant activation};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\medskip
\centering
\chip{what every theorem on this line gives}{existence for any tolerance}\quad\chip{what none of them gives}{a rate, a width, a slope, or a training method}
\note{
Weierstrass in 1885 proved that polynomials come within any tolerance of every continuous function on an interval.
Stone in 1937 extended the theorem to any algebra of continuous functions on a compact set that separates points and contains the constants, so the trigonometric polynomials and many other families are dense as well.
Kolmogorov in 1957 wrote every continuous function of several variables as a finite sum of compositions of continuous functions of one variable, a two-layer form.

Cybenko in 1989 proved that networks with one hidden layer of sigmoid units are dense in the continuous functions on a box, and Hornik in 1991 showed that any bounded nonconstant activation gives the same result.
Every theorem on this line has the form Weierstrass gave it, existence for any tolerance, with no rate, no width, and nothing about slopes or training.
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Positive definiteness of a kernel}
\begin{columns}
\column{0.5\textwidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small]
\draw[ink, thick] (0,0) -- (6,0);
\foreach \x/\l in {0.5/$0$, 2.9/$0.6\ell$, 5.3/$1.2\ell$} {\fill[sblue] (\x,0) circle (3pt); \node[below] at (\x,-0.1) {\l};}
\draw[sgreen, thick] (0.5,0.35) to[bend left=35] node[above, font=\scriptsize] {similar, $1$} (2.9,0.35);
\draw[sgreen, thick] (2.9,0.35) to[bend left=35] node[above, font=\scriptsize] {similar, $1$} (5.3,0.35);
\draw[sred, thick, dashed] (0.5,-0.7) to[bend right=25] node[below, font=\scriptsize] {unrelated, $0$} (5.3,-0.7);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\[
\mathbf{K} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda = 1 - \sqrt2,\ 1,\ 1 + \sqrt2
\]
\column{0.5\textwidth}
\[
\mathbf{w} = (1, -\sqrt2, 1), \qquad \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w} = -1.66
\]
\medskip
\[
k(x, x') = \phi(x)^\top\phi(x') \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w} = \Bigl\|\sum_i w_i\,\phi(x_i)\Bigr\|^2 \ge 0
\]
\end{columns}
\note{
Could we have picked any similarity rule?
Try one.
Put sensors at $0$, $0.6\ell$, $1.2\ell$, and call two of them similar, with value one, when closer than $\ell$ and unrelated otherwise.
The matrix has eigenvalues $1 - \sqrt2 = -0.414$, $1$, and $1 + \sqrt2$, and the weights $(1, -\sqrt2, 1)$ give $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w} = -1.66$.
A negative value cannot be a squared length, so no inner product produces this rule.

A kernel is positive definite when $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w} \ge 0$ for every choice of sites and weights.
Whenever $k(x, x') = \phi(x)^\top\phi(x')$ for some list of features, $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w} = \|\sum_i w_i \phi(x_i)\|^2 \ge 0$, and every positive definite kernel has this form with the list allowed to be infinite.
So a kernel method is linear regression on features we never write down.
}
\end{frame}

\[
\langle k(x_1, \cdot), k(x_2, \cdot)\rangle_k = k(x_1, x_2)

f(x) = \langle f, k(x, \cdot)\rangle_k
\]

\end{columns}
\note{
Now make the kernel's geometry explicit.
Declare the inner product of two kernel copies to be their similarity, $\langle k(x_1, \cdot), k(x_2, \cdot)\rangle_k = k(x_1, x_2)$, extend it by linearity, and complete the collection.
That is the reproducing kernel Hilbert space, and its overlap differs from the $L^2$ overlap, $0.61$ against $0.78$ for two unit Gaussian bumps a distance $\ell$ apart.

Take a member, three weighted copies with $\ell = 0.16$, $f = 0.8\,k(0.2, \cdot) - 0.45\,k(0.5, \cdot) + 0.65\,k(0.8, \cdot)$.
Its squared norm is $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha} = 1.041$, the similarities of the centers weighted by the coefficients.

Pair $f$ with the copy centered at $x^* = 0.72$, the dotted curve.
The rule returns $0.8\,k(0.2, x^*) - 0.45\,k(0.5, x^*) + 0.65\,k(0.8, x^*)$, which is $f(x^*) = 0.403$, and in general $f(x) = \langle f, k(x, \cdot)\rangle_k$ for every member.
Evaluating at a point is an inner product, continuous by Cauchy--Schwarz, $|f(x)| \le \|f\|_k\sqrt{k(x,x)} = 1.02$ here, which is what $L^2$ could not give us.
Differentiate the identity and the slope is bounded too, $|f'(x)| \le \|f\|_k/\ell$, so a function that climbs by $1$ over $\ell/10$ has norm at least $10$.
The norm measures roughness, and we are about to penalize it.
}
\end{frame}

The interpolant reproduces every reading, noise included.
How bad is that?
At overlap $0.99$ the two-sensor matrix has eigenvalues $1.99$ and $0.01$, along $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$, and a reading pair $(1,1)$ gives weights near one half each.

A noise pair $(\varepsilon, -\varepsilon)$, the difference between two nearly equal readings, gives weights $\pm\varepsilon/0.01$, a hundred times the noise.
The hundredfold is in the coefficients along $\phi_1 - \phi_2$, whose function has norm $0.14$, so the curve moves by fourteen times the noise while its coefficients move by a hundred.

We have changed the question.
Approximating a known function asked which member is closest.
Recovering an unknown function from noisy readings asks whether the readings identify it, and interpolation answers by trusting every reading to the last digit.
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Kernel ridge regression and the choice of $\lambda$}
\begin{columns}
\column{0.5\textwidth}
\[
\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}_k} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(f(x_i) - y_i\bigr)^2 + \lambda \|f\|_k^2
\]
\[
(\mathbf{K} + \lambda\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \mathbf{y}
\]
\medskip
\centering
\chip{$\lambda = 0.1$, small divisor}{$0.01 \to 0.11$}\quad\chip{amplification}{a hundred $\to$ nine}
\column{0.5\textwidth}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/ridge-ucurve}
\end{figure}
\end{columns}
\note{
So add a roughness penalty to the misfit and minimize $\sum_i (f(x_i) - y_i)^2 + \lambda\|f\|_k^2$ over the whole space.
Any component of $f$ orthogonal to the span of the data's kernel copies changes no reading and raises the norm, so the minimizer lies in that span, and substituting gives $(\mathbf{K} + \lambda\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \mathbf{y}$, the interpolation solve with the identity added to the diagonal.
At $\lambda = 0.1$ the small divisor rises from $0.01$ to $0.11$ and the amplification drops from a hundred to nine.

The figure shows twenty noisy sine samples across nine decades of $\lambda$.
Near-interpolation reaches ninety times the best error, the minimum sits near $10^{-1}$, and over-smoothing rises beyond it.
The matched weight $\lambda = \sigma^2 = 10^{-2}$ is one decade below this sample's minimum, and in practice we choose $\lambda$ by predicting held-out readings.
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conditioning a Gaussian on an observation}
\begin{columns}
\column{0.55\textwidth}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figs/rep-conditioning}
\end{figure}
\column{0.45\textwidth}
\[
f(x_1) = Z_1, \qquad f(x_2) = \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\, Z_2
\]
\[
f(x_2) \mid f(x_1) = y \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\bigl(\rho y,\ 1 - \rho^2\bigr)
\]
\medskip
\chip{$\rho = 0.9$}{mean $0.9y$, standard deviation $0.44$}\\[4pt]
\chip{$\rho = 0.3$}{mean $0.3y$, standard deviation $0.95$}
\end{columns}
\note{
The same computation reads as probability.
Build two temperatures from independent standard normals, $f(x_1) = Z_1$ and $f(x_2) = \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\,Z_2$, so both have variance one and covariance $\rho$.

Observe $f(x_1) = y$.
That fixes $Z_1 = y$, and $f(x_2) = \rho y + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z_2$ is Gaussian with mean $\rho y$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$.
At $\rho = 0.9$ the slice has mean $0.9$ and standard deviation $0.44$, at $\rho = 0.3$ it keeps mean $0.3$ and standard deviation $0.95$.

A Gaussian process makes any finite list of values jointly Gaussian with covariances $k(x_i, x_j)$, and this is the case $n = 2$.
}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Kolmogorov $n$-width}
\begin{columns}
\column{0.4\textwidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, >={Stealth[length=2.5mm]}, font=\small]
\draw[->, ink] (0,0) -- (2.4,0) node[right] {$(1,0,0)$};
\draw[->, ink] (0,0) -- (0,2.4) node[above] {$(0,1,0)$};
\draw[->, ink] (0,0) -- (-1.5,-1.3) node[below left] {$(0,0,1)$};
\fill[sblue!20, opacity=0.8] (-1.2,0.9) -- (2.2,1.7) -- (1.6,-1.2) -- (-1.8,-2.0) -- cycle;
\node[sblue, font=\scriptsize] at (1.2,1.55) {a plane, two dimensions};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\column{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{block}{Kolmogorov $n$-width}
\[
d_n(\mathcal{M}) = \min_{\dim V = n} \; \max_{f \in \mathcal{M}} \; \min_{v \in V} \|f - v\|
\]
\end{block}
\medskip
\chip{three unit spikes, squared distances to any plane}{sum to $3 - 2 = 1$}\quad\chip{worst spike}{at least $\sqrt{1/3} = 0.577$}
\end{columns}
\note{
How well can any fixed basis do on a whole family?
Translate a unit spike across three grid points, so the snapshots are three perpendicular unit vectors.
For any plane through the origin the three squared distances to it add up to $3 - 2 = 1$, so the worst snapshot sits at distance at least $\sqrt{1/3} = 0.577$, whichever plane we choose.
A family that depends on one number needs all three dimensions.

The Kolmogorov $n$-width is the worst-case error of the best $n$-dimensional subspace.
Read it from the inside out.
The innermost minimum is the projection theorem, the maximum takes the hardest member, and the outer minimum designs the subspace with full knowledge of the family.
No fixed basis of size $n$ has a smaller worst-case error.
}
\end{frame}

We stored a function as a list of numbers and a rule, readings joined by segments or coefficients on shapes, and a fixed family was exact on its members and approximate outside them.
We measured error in a norm the physics chose, and two functions could be close in one norm and far apart in another without bound.
We found the closest member of a family by making the error orthogonal to the family, one linear solve, and saw that the inner product was a choice.
Coefficients on a basis fell at a rate set by the smoothness of the function relative to the shapes, and a jump left an overshoot that never converged away in the sup norm.
Weierstrass gave density with no rate, and existence of a closest member needed a complete space and a closed family.
$L^2$ had no point values and $H^1$ restored them in one dimension, so kernels could turn scattered readings into a curve with a norm that measures roughness and a band conditional on the model.
A family of functions had a width that bounded every fixed basis at once, and particles, trained shapes, and selection dropped the fixed-basis hypothesis in three ways.
A hat is three ReLU ramps, so the straight-segment guess was already a network, and training moves its breakpoints.
